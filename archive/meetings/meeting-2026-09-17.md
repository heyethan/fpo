# Meetings, 2026-09-17

Two sessions, back to back. First with Shobith, an outside advisor. Second was the four
founders alone, walking the convergence/divergence map.

Speaker labels in the source transcript were scrambled — one label covered several people,
and Shobith and Aneesh never appeared correctly. Ethan confirmed every attribution below.

## Session 1 — with Shobith

Shobith joined as an outside voice. He is a product of InUnity (Affinity) and knows the
local incubation scene from the inside.

### What Adithya opened with

A community, not a consultancy. The group is exclusive, and the exclusivity has to be
earned by two things: businesses and ideas that hold a moat rather than being a better
copy of something existing, and access to talent. He flagged it as a direction, not a
decision — "these are things we haven't resolved."

### What Shobith pushed on

**Narrow the verticals.** Pick a top three or five and name them. "What we are good at" is
still too general a filter. He pointed at Rainmatter — climate, agri, IoT — as a group that
runs a community well by staying narrow. Avoid AI/ML: too crowded to win partnerships in.
Industry 4.0 has untapped avenues, including funding routes.

Adithya had already applied one filter: no domains the group doesn't know, because entering
one without knowledge or curriculum costs too much.

**Start small.** Five to ten companies, worked closely for six months. Six months is enough
for a company to get a read on PMF. Ten companies worked deeply beats a wide open cohort.

**Distribution over funding.** Any student picked will know how to build a product. The two
blockers are funds and distribution. Asked to choose one, Shobith took distribution without
hesitation — products get built without close mentoring, but first customers don't appear on
their own. Get them their first five leads, or even one or two.

The long game: FPO's own name has to *become* the distribution, the way YC's does. A YC
badge is automatic distribution, and investors' names carry the same weight when a company
talks to clients. So there are two tracks — the cohort, and FPO building its own credibility
so it can hand distribution to anyone inside it.

**Curriculum as a short handbook.** Not long. First principles, a linear loop: take a
problem, validate, build, validate again, iterate. Weekly reviews to unblock people going
directionally wrong. If the cohort lacks the basics, spend the first two months on case
studies — how different products get built, how different industries are read, 0 to 1 and
1 to 10 — then move on.

### The college

St Aloysius is the first approach: autonomous, flexible, faculty the group trusts, and
lecturers who already run companies. SJEC doesn't have that flexibility.

Ethan's read is that Aloysius students are stuck further back than distribution. They can't
ideate, can't research or validate an idea, and don't know how to kill the ideas that
should die. Nobody has taught them that.

Arjun's framing: the first cohort is an experiment on known people with known ideas —
lecturers and their companies — not students arriving with their own.

### Funds

Available, not deployed. Ethan has access and doesn't plan to use it for this cohort unless
something exceptional shows up.

Adithya argued against injecting funds into ideas at all. In a tier-2 city, doing this in
your penultimate year already cuts against the stigma of getting a job and settling down, so
the conservative route works better: a respectable stipend or salary for founders in the
cohort, a psychological incentive, rather than seed money they may not know how to deploy.
Ethan named the shape: entrepreneur in residence.

**No equity at the start.** Adithya: incubation centres that claim equity early end up
holding nothing, because there's no moat and no valuation at T+1 or T+2. Arjun added that
incorporation is nine to twelve months out anyway, and not marrying people to FPO that
early leaves both sides free to walk. Win-win either way.

### The investor question

Ethan asked what the group would say if an investor joined the call in ten minutes, and what
the answer looks like if the money goes in and the ROI is terrible.

Adithya's line: **"Essentially, we are a broker."** A broker for sellers of shares in
companies that have value, catering to funds and investment bankers in early-stage. FPO
surfaces startups that exist in a tier-2 city but never reach LinkedIn or the front page.

On ticket size, Adithya was blunt about the market: early-stage funds are getting roughly
one deal every six months, and that deal competes across every cohort run like this one. A
fund won't go past about $10k at this stage; the realistic alternative is angels, which
incubators already have. The spike comes at early-to-Series-A. Ethan's own contacts start
at $100k minimum, and a Mumbai contact runs an incubation programme with ~47 companies.

**Success metric: unresolved.** Adithya pressed it — funding coming in can't be the metric,
so what is? Ethan offered startups that come out with a better idea than they came in with,
and called it a humble goal. Left open.

### Parallel track: existing companies

Adithya proposed also taking on companies that already have traction, rather than only
finding students and handing them ideas. Less resource per company, and more confidence in
front of the next investor. Thresholds: B2B, three to five pilots lined up. B2C, roughly
100 to 500 users. Traction, not revenue.

Runs in parallel with the first cohort, and feeds the distribution side.

### Three tiers

Arjun's framing, and the one that settled the founder-versus-talent question from the intake:

1. Entrepreneur — best case.
2. Can't do that but strong technically — researcher.
3. Neither — skilled workforce, get a job, be good at it.

"You join where you think you belong, we'll throw you where we think you belong."

This also answers the BEBA question Ethan raised: the cohort doesn't displace the research
culture, it runs parallel and feeds it. People who execute well but don't want to scale a
business get groomed toward research instead — "we worked with you this long, this didn't
work for you, here's what we can do for you." Adithya agreed: if not a founder, then good
talent. Both are the expected outcome.

### Timing and noise

No PR, no marketing, no building in public yet. Adithya: right thing, wrong stage — wait
until the pointers are clear, then build a story around them.

No investor pitches for about three months either. Arjun was firm, citing the Eagle 10
meeting: "I don't want to create a list of people I need to apologise to." If they sell
Mangalore as crazy brains and district toppers, then turn up with a hundred calculator apps,
the credibility is gone. Convincing smart investors is the easy part — do it for the right
reasons, once there's a cohort to show.

Ethan's position: don't tell them anything now. At three months, with results, open the
conversation.

### Shobith's role

Advisory for the next few months. Hop on calls, discuss progress, give feedback — but more
involved in the live conversation than a standard mentor. Roughly biweekly, sometimes a
fifteen-minute check, sometimes nothing that week. Not hands-on yet; Ethan's read is it's
too early and the founders have to figure it out first. Shobith agreed. His strength is
product, and he offered curriculum design and mentoring when the time comes. He asked for
it to be set up more formally — "give me just 10 points."

Most of the founders are travelling this quarter. Ethan is the one on the ground. Shobith is
in town for the next two months, then travelling for one.

## Session 2 — the four founders, walking the map

### Settled

**1. Not a nonprofit.** The not-for-profit idea is dropped. "It would be an FPO, as the
group name suggests." This closes the biggest split in the intake.

**2. Two programs, not one.** It need not be one integrated program. Two separate tracks
running in parallel.

**3. Stage: prototyping.** Ethan and Adithya had said building, Arjun and Aneesh had said
idea. Ethan called it semantics — ideated, and now building while still ideating. Everyone
converged, and Arjun landed the word: not building, *prototyping*. In between the two.

**4. What FPO stands for — not a split.** The four intake answers are complementary, not
divergent. Adithya's structure: "entrepreneurial and research growth" is the vision
statement, and the three other answers are the mission — individual functions under a broad
umbrella. Arjun's one-sentence attempt: entrepreneurial and research growth, staying with
potential, enabling them to build companies.

**5. The problem, in one line.** Four intake answers collapsed into Adithya's: *creating
efficient and employable youth* — flip it and that's the problem statement. Ethan accepted
it while noting the original four are facets of it. Nobody offered a competing sentence.

**6. Competitors — not a split either.** Different altitudes, not disagreement. Ethan reads
competitors at the top tier and named Wispr Flow as the aspiration, for the community they
built across every subdomain. Cosh and DT Labz are competitors at this stage. InUnity is a
competitor now. DK24 isn't a competitor but can't be ignored, because they've established
something FPO hasn't — and the way past them is value first, not volume first. IIC is
national, running KTECH-style events, not the Aloysius body.

### Open

**The InUnity question.** Arjun's, and the sharpest thing in either session. He was in
InUnity's cohort — his idea got selected. He walked the group through their playbook:
design-thinking workshops for a couple of weeks, in-person problem-statement sessions,
generic SIP problems handed to groups whose ideas were rejected, ideation, stakeholder
interviews, primary and secondary research, build, demo, grand finale. Three or four
companies incorporated out of it, Shobith's Affinity among them. Tailored curriculum,
mentoring, weekly check-ins, loud PR about results. Second cohort was only okay.

His question: **how are we different? What is our innovation here?** Doing the same
blueprint better isn't a bad plan and he isn't contesting that FPO could out-execute them —
but if they want to succeed they need to know what was actually missing, not assume better
execution covers it.

The answer offered was that the metric is the end result, which Arjun correctly read as
"get the first cohort through and see." He asked for real analysis of what each competitor
did and where it broke, framed explicitly as brand work, not as disparagement.

Ethan's call: this matters and will be done, but at the point in the branding process where
it belongs. The flow comes first.

**The quadrant dots.** Two founders contested their placement.

- Aneesh: should have been on the company/commercial side, doesn't know what put him toward
  community. More fundamentally, he rejects the axis — commercial and community aren't
  opposites, and treating them as poles splits things that belong together. His own
  priority order: company, founders, then commercial, then community.
- Ethan: stands where he is, but the reason isn't the NPO answers Adithya suggested. It's
  that he cares about the community itself — who comes in, who goes out, what happens in the
  cohort, what comes out of it. He wants to be the one building it.
- Arjun: stands by his placement. Commercial because FPO, youth-enabler because of his
  written answers. Sees it as a commercial project.

The axis itself is now in question, not just the dots on it.

**Known-against list.** Adithya moved to retract his entry — telling founders early there's
no business, and mentorship past the cohort — on the grounds that curriculum design overlaps
with what he was criticising. Arjun and Ethan disagreed that it dissed anything, and he let
it stand. Ethan and Aneesh read as aligned here; Arjun's is broader; Adithya's was the most
specific.
