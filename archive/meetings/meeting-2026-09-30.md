## The three questions, answered

### 1. Selectivity is how the reputation is earned, not the reputation itself

The exercise-two map had selectivity as the live candidate for what FPO is known for. The room
demoted it.

**Adithya**, who proposed selectivity in the written round, argued it down himself:

> "It is how the reputation gets earned. We don't want to be selective, and that's not our USP. We
> want everybody to have a fair shot, but who we mentor is up to us. So then how we have mentored
> them becomes the game. The reputation itself is not that we select the best people."

The argument that settled it: if selectivity were the reputation, it would not have to be
merit-based. You can be selective on friendship, institution or community and still be selective.
The word carries no promise on its own.

**What FPO is known for is the quality of the mentoring.** Selectivity is the means.

Adithya has now withdrawn both of his own candidates: reliability on 2026-09-28, and selectivity
here. In both cases the objection was the same, that the word does not survive contact with how it
would actually be earned.

### 2. No second pond. The problem side is unbounded.

The map claimed FPO had two ponds, a local one for talent and an unbounded one for problem
suppliers. The room rejected both the claim and the framing.

**Arjun:** "I don't see a point of why we need to call them a pond at all. Wherever the problems
come from, they're valued for us. I agree with the point, but I don't see why it needs to be made
a point." And: "The pond for sourcing talent completely makes sense, but there is no pond for
getting problems fed. It's the ocean. It's infinite. We can get it from anywhere." His summary of
the map's framing: an unnecessary restriction.

Settled: talent is Mangalore and coastal Karnataka. Problems come from anywhere. The relationship
is concentric circles rather than two categories.

**Adithya raised a real follow-up**, open rather than settled: whether stakeholders act as a filter
on where problems come from. If FPO works with an institution, a municipal body or a government
department, do they get a say in which problems FPO takes? His own answer was that it narrows
things, and he flagged the tension against a small cohort of limited expertise.

**The answer that emerged, from the Karthik conversation:** a problem FPO cannot handle today
routes to a partner entity, DevVoid, CodexCape, Evlogia, or Adithya's future law firm, and because
the deal came through FPO a portion comes back and is reinvested. Where FPO can take it, FPO takes
it. Other projects are welcome regardless, because they build relationships and give the cohort
real work to train on.

### 3. Who pays between the first cohort and the first cash flow

**Sponsors, brand deals and customers.** Nobody pays out of pocket, neither the founders nor an
investor being burned.

**Arjun** reconstructed the full answer:

> "Sponsors and brand deals are paying for the first cohort, but we are not spending extravagantly.
> Fair pay would not mean you get paid your hourly rate, whatever you're expecting to make a
> profit. You give a discounted rate to begin with to set up a relationship, even if it's our plus
> one who we are bringing into the FPO. So we spend minimally. We cut costs, cut corners if
> required, but the money has to come from brand deals and the sponsors and the customers."

**Research projects pay on success.** Until that money arrives, the service projects fund
everything. When it does arrive, the redistribution formula has to be written; it does not exist
yet.

**Rate cards are needed for anyone brought in from outside**, not for the four founders.

**On taking a cut from cohort companies:** not at the start. Let them reinvest as long as they
need. If FPO is genuinely funding them, terms can be set later against milestones, for example a
threshold of paying customers before anything comes back.
